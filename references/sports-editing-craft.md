# Sports editing craft

## Selection and narrative

Keep anticipation, peak action, and enough aftermath to understand what happened. A finish without the assist, a tackle without the approach, or a shot without its outcome may be visually exciting but incomplete. Adapt handles to the sport and shot; do not use a universal number of seconds. Avoid cutting away at ball contact, release or landing merely to hit a musical beat.

Build intensity through stronger plays, contrasting shot sizes, reactions and appropriate pauses. A complete game recap may need chronology; a montage may reorder distinct plays, but must not imply they happened as one continuous sequence. Separate replays and alternative angles with a clear visual cue when confusion is likely. Preserve decisive action at normal speed somewhere in athlete evaluation/showcase material.

## Reframing and tracking

Identify the intended athlete from user-provided or corroborated source context. Track the action's path and destination, not just a face or torso. Keep the ball/puck, relevant opponents, goal/basket/finish line and landing area visible when they explain the play. Anticipate camera pans and leave lead room.

For landscape-to-portrait conversion, test the hardest action segment before committing to a crop. Use smooth, bounded keyframes without abrupt whip pans or jitter. If portrait cannot convey a wide play, use a meaningful multi-view composition or landscape deliverable rather than clipping the decisive event. Do not stretch footage or fill missing action with generated imagery.

## Speed and effects

Probe true source frame rate and review motion cadence. Slow motion is limited by captured frames: prefer genuine high-frame-rate footage. If interpolation is requested, inspect limbs, balls, nets and fast occlusions for warping, and record that frames were synthesized. Do not assume optical flow is harmless.

Use speed ramps around anticipation and recovery; preserve readable contact, release and outcome. Map speed changes into the actual output timeline for captions, audio and graphics. Freeze frames, tracking markers and brief zooms can identify an athlete before the play. Keep them purposeful and away from critical action.

Use restraint with shake, flashing, transitions and blur; an energetic edit should still make the sport readable. Avoid repetitive strobing. Cut or transition between plays on movement or sound where it supports the sequence. Do not use an effect to conceal a broken edit.

## Audio

Use supplied or appropriately authorized music. Align selected action peaks with musical accents while maintaining play continuity. Preserve useful crowd reactions, shoe squeaks, ball impact and commentary. Duck music under important speech; avoid clipping or pumping. Do not add fake commentary or impact sounds that materially misrepresent an event. Treat stylized sound design as an effect, not evidence.

Choose and measure an output loudness target appropriate to the delivery brief. Verify final peaks after effects and music are mixed. Listen around ramps and transitions for pitch artifacts, abrupt ambience and clicks.
